Reproducing the study

This page explains how to train a policy for the hydrothermal case and how to compare it with the baselines, so that a reader can obtain results of the same quality as those reported for the method, although not the same numbers. The stage problems are nonconvex AC power flows solved to local optimality, the policy is a deep recurrent network trained by stochastic gradient descent, and the interior-point solver, the sampled trajectories and the GPU arithmetic all influence where a run comes to rest. Two runs of the same schedule reach different local optima of similar cost, and comparisons are meaningful at that resolution. The commands live in the examples README and in the READMEs of examples/HydroPowerModels in this package and in DecisionRulesExa.jl; this page explains the choices behind them.

What is fixed

The case is frozen and verified. A manifest records the hash of every input file and the invariants derived from them (the weekly water balance, the load factor, the price of shedding load, the empty initial reservoirs), and both engines refuse to build a stage model from a case that does not verify. Policies are trained over 126 weekly stages and evaluated over the first 96, so that the reported window is free of the end-of-horizon dumping of water that any finite horizon invites.

Evaluation uses one shared protocol of 500 paired inflow scenarios, generated from a fixed seed rather than stored. Every method is scored on the same scenarios, so a comparison between any two policies is a paired comparison: the difference in their costs scenario by scenario has a spread far smaller than the spread of either cost, which is what makes small differences decidable with a few hundred scenarios. A policy evaluated by either engine on this protocol is directly comparable to every other policy evaluated on it.

Training as a deep-learning problem

Training a policy over a long horizon with nonconvex stage problems is a difficult deep-learning problem, and it requires the corresponding practices. A single uninterrupted run with one learning-rate schedule rarely reaches a good policy. Training is instead organized in phases, each a separate process that restarts from the checkpoint the previous phase selected, with a fresh optimizer state and a fresh learning-rate schedule, as described in Training, and why it is staged. Each phase saves the best policy found on the selection panel and a recovery snapshot of its full state, so an interrupted phase continues its own trajectory instead of starting over. A restart of the learning-rate schedule temporarily increases the cost before decreasing it; a stopping rule that does not account for this terminates the phase prematurely, which is why the stale-evaluation stop is off by default.

The default schedule, declared in lineage_from_scratch.json and executed by run_tsddr_lineage.jl, is a good start rather than a tuned optimum. It begins with a small number of sampled trajectories per update and a high learning rate, which gives cheap and noisy gradients suited to fast descent, and ends with more trajectories and a lower learning rate, which gives the precise gradient the last part of the descent needs. On a different machine, a different budget or a modified case, the same principle applies with different values: when the selection metric stops improving, either the step is too large for the precision of the gradient, and the learning rate should fall, or the gradient is too noisy for the step, and the sample size should grow.

The baselines

SDDP is trained by sddp/run_sddp_inconsistent.jl, which builds cuts on a convex relaxation of the network and runs its forward pass on the true AC model. The default backward model is the SOC-WR relaxation; DR_SDDP_BACKWARD=dc builds the cuts on the linearized DC network instead, solved with Gurobi. SDDP is run until its bound has stopped moving and its forward costs have settled, and its policy is then evaluated with the cuts frozen on the paired protocol, exactly like a learned policy.

The linear decision rule of the two-stage linear decision-rule literature is trained with the same driver and the same schedule as TS-DDR, from lineage_tsldr_from_scratch.json; only the architecture differs, so the comparison isolates the value of the nonlinear policy. The reinforcement-learning baselines in examples/HydroPowerModels/rl (REINFORCE, PPO, TD3 and SAC) act in an environment that wraps the same strict stage problems and the same reachable target map, and are trained on the stage cost alone. They isolate the value of the dual gradient as the learning signal. Their environment should be checked against a TS-DDR checkpoint with the parity mode of train_rl.jl before any training budget is spent.

Practical lessons

Verify the complete policy gradient against finite differences before a long run. A truncated gradient still trains and still lowers the loss; it descends in the wrong direction, and every hyperparameter conclusion drawn on top of it is wrong too. The check is described in The gradient must flow through the reachable map.

Keep the training signal and the selection signal apart. The training loss is one noisy sample over the full training horizon; the selection metric is a fixed panel over the reported horizon, and only the latter should select a checkpoint. Select on complete evaluations only, because a mean over a scenario that failed to solve is a mean over a different denominator. Finally, report a policy on a protocol it was never selected on, and count the phases that produced nothing when stating how long training took.
