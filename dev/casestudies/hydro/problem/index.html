The long-term hydrothermal planning problem

Long-term hydrothermal dispatch determines, at weekly stages over a horizon of several years, the turbined outflow and spillage of every hydro plant and the dispatch of the thermal units, so as to minimize the expected operating cost subject to the reservoir dynamics, the network constraints and the uncertainty of inflows. Hydro generation has no direct cost. Its economic value is the opportunity cost of the stored water, the expected thermal cost that the water displaces in later stages, and this value depends on the distribution of future inflows and on the capacity of the network to deliver the energy to the loads.

This page states the problem as the case study solves it: the reservoir dynamics, the conversion of water into power on an AC network, the planning problem and the value of water it defines, the stage problem that a target-state policy solves, and the instance. How each method values water is the subject of Valuing water: two approaches.

Reservoirs and cascades

Time is divided into weekly stages $t = 1, \ldots, T$. The state is the vector of reservoir volumes $x_t = (v_{r,t})_{r \in \mathcal{R}}$, and the uncertainty revealed at the start of each stage is the vector of river inflows $w_t = (w_{r,t})_{r \in \mathcal{R}}$. Within the stage the operator chooses the turbined outflow $q_{r,t}$ and the spillage $s_{r,t}$ of every plant, the thermal dispatch, and the operating point of the network. Rivers form cascades: the water released by an upstream plant reaches the plant below it within the same week, so the volume of each reservoir evolves by the water balance

\[v_{r,t} \;=\; v_{r,t-1} + K \Bigl( w_{r,t} - q_{r,t} + \sum_{u \in \mathcal{U}_r} q_{u,t} \Bigr) - s_{r,t} + \sum_{u \in \mathcal{S}_r} s_{u,t}, \qquad \underline{v}_r \le v_{r,t} \le \overline{v}_r ,\]

where $\mathcal{U}_r$ and $\mathcal{S}_r$ are the plants whose turbined and spilled water, respectively, flow into reservoir $r$, and $\underline{q}_r \le q_{r,t} \le \overline{q}_r$, $s_{r,t} \ge 0$. The factor $K$ converts a flow sustained over the stage into a volume and is proportional to the stage length; for a week of 168 hours it is $K = 0.0036 \times 168 = 0.6048$. Inflow and turbined flow are rates while spillage is carried as a volume, the convention of HydroPowerModels that every engine of the case study reproduces. The water balance is the only constraint that couples consecutive stages: everything else the operator faces is contained within the week.

From water to electricity

A hydro plant converts turbined flow into active power through its production factor, $p_{r,t} = \rho_r\, q_{r,t}$. Production factors differ by nearly an order of magnitude across the plants of the case study, and a plant at the head of a cascade passes its released water to the plant below, which turbines it again. A volume of water therefore does not correspond to a fixed quantity of energy: its value depends on where it is stored and on the plants it will pass through.

Within each stage the dispatch satisfies the AC power-flow equations on the transmission network. With bus voltages $|V_i| e^{j\varphi_i}$ and the conductance and susceptance $G_{ik}, B_{ik}$ of the bus admittance matrix, the active and reactive balances at bus $i$ read

\[\begin{aligned} \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}_i} p_{g,t} + \sum_{r \in \mathcal{R}_i} p_{r,t} - P^{d}_{i,t} + \Delta_{i,t} &= |V_i| \sum_{k} |V_k| \bigl( G_{ik} \cos\varphi_{ik} + B_{ik} \sin\varphi_{ik} \bigr), \\ \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}_i} q_{g,t} - Q^{d}_{i,t} &= |V_i| \sum_{k} |V_k| \bigl( G_{ik} \sin\varphi_{ik} - B_{ik} \cos\varphi_{ik} \bigr), \end{aligned}\]

with $\varphi_{ik} = \varphi_i - \varphi_k$, together with voltage bands, apparent-power limits at both ends of every branch, and generator capabilities. These equations are nonconvex in the voltages, and through them losses, reactive support and voltage limits make the cost of delivery depend on where power is produced and consumed. The variable $\Delta_{i,t} \ge 0$ is the unserved load at bus $i$, priced far above the most expensive generator. It gives every stage relatively complete recourse in the active-power balance: for every incoming state and every reachable target, the stage problem remains feasible. Spillage is unbounded, so no slack is needed on the other side. The reactive balance has no slack and is enforced exactly.

The planning problem and the value of water

The stage cost is the fuel cost of the thermal units plus the cost of unserved load,

\[c_t(x_t, u_t) \;=\; \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}} C_g\bigl(p_{g,t}\bigr) + C_{\Delta} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}} \Delta_{i,t},\]

and hydro generation costs nothing at the stage level. Its cost is the opportunity cost of the water withheld, which appears only through the intertemporal coupling. The planning problem is the instance of Multistage stochastic optimization in which the operator chooses a nonanticipative policy to minimize the expected total cost over the horizon, subject to the water balance and the AC equations at every stage. Its dynamic programming formulation defines a cost-to-go $V_{t+1}(x_t)$, and the negative of its gradient with respect to the volume of reservoir $r$, $-\partial\, \mathbb{E}[V_{t+1}] / \partial v_{r,t}$, is the marginal value of water in that reservoir.

The water value has a temporal, a spatial and a stochastic component. Water released in one week is unavailable in later weeks, and when the demand peak falls in the dry season, as in the Bolivian system, the stages in which water is most valuable are those in which inflows are lowest. Through the production factors and the cascade topology, the same volume corresponds to different amounts of energy at different plants, and the network constraints determine how much of that energy reaches the loads. And every release is decided before the next inflow is observed, so an optimal policy hedges against the distribution of inflows rather than against a single forecast. A myopic policy, which assigns zero value to stored water, ignores all three components.

The policy implementation problem

A target-state policy does not decide releases. At every stage it emits a target $\hat v_{r,t}$ for the end-of-week volume of every reservoir, and the stage problem dispatches the system with the volume pinned to that target, $v_{r,t} = \hat v_{r,t}$. The multiplier $\lambda_{r,t}$ of this equality is the sensitivity of the stage cost to the target, a price for water read directly from the AC problem; Valuing water: two approaches explains how it trains the policy. The equality is well posed only if the target can actually be reached in one week from the incoming volume, so the policy must emit targets inside the one-stage reachable set.

One-stage reachable sets

The water balance is linear in the releases, so the set of volumes that reservoir $r$ can reach in one stage is an interval, obtained by setting the releases at their bounds:

\[u_r = \min\Bigl(\overline{v}_r,\; v_r + K w_r - K \underline{q}_r + \sum_{u \in \mathcal{U}_r} K \overline{q}_u\Bigr), \qquad \ell_r = \max\bigl(\underline{v}_r,\; v_r + K w_r - K \overline{q}_r - \overline{s}_r\bigr),\]

with $\ell_r = \underline{v}_r$ when spillage is unbounded, since a reservoir can always be drawn down to its minimum. The policy maps an unconstrained output $z_r$ of the network into this interval, $\hat v_r = \ell_r + (u_r - \ell_r)\,\sigma(z_r)$. The bounds depend on the incoming volume and on the realized inflow, and because the incoming volume is the previous stage's target, they are part of what couples the stages in the policy's gradient.

The upper bound assumes that every upstream plant releases at its turbine capacity. When an upstream reservoir holds water back, less reaches the reservoir below, and a downstream target computed from the capacity bound could be unreachable. The policy therefore processes the reservoirs in topological order along each cascade. For a link $u \to d$, the release implied by the upstream target is $R_u = K w_u + v_u - \hat v_u$; the contribution reaching $d$ is $\max(0, R_u)$ when both turbined and spilled water flow downstream, and $\min(K \overline{q}_u, \max(0, R_u))$ when only turbined water does; and the downstream target is limited to

\[\hat v_d \;\le\; \min\bigl(\overline{v}_d,\; v_d + K w_d - K \underline{q}_d + \text{contribution}_{u \to d}\bigr).\]

This construction is exact for cascades of one level, the topology of the Bolivian system, and conservative for longer chains. With it, every target the policy emits is reachable from the state it was conditioned on, and by induction the whole target trajectory is feasible (see Validity in every formulation, by induction).

The Bolivian system

The instance is the Bolivian interconnected system: 28 buses, 31 branches and 34 generators, of which 11 are hydro units arranged in three cascades. The network is meshed, but its three loops close only through lower-voltage lines in the central mining region, and the transfers from the hydro plants in the north-west and the gas plants in the east to the loads follow essentially radial corridors.

Two of the cascade links carry both turbined and spilled water downstream; on the third, only the turbined water of the system's one large seasonal reservoir reaches a run-of-river plant immediately below it. Every unit of water released at the head of that cascade is therefore converted twice, and its marginal value includes both production factors. Inflows are strongly seasonal and spatially correlated across the basins; they are modelled as stage-wise independent, with equiprobable joint realizations per week built from the historical record, and they are the only source of uncertainty. Demand follows a deterministic profile whose peak falls in the dry season, so that the comparison concerns only the valuation of water.

Policies are trained and simulated over 126 weeks and scored over the first 96, so that the additional weeks remove the incentive to empty the reservoirs at the end of the reported horizon. The reservoirs start empty, and unserved load is priced at 6000 per per-unit (100 MW) of power and stage. The case files, their hashes and the invariants derived from them are recorded in a manifest that both engines verify before building a model.
